How the data were put together

  • Years. FY 2012 to FY 2024. All of these years use the same survey design, introduced in FY 2010, and include the short-form survey for institutions with less than $1 million in R&D, so national totals are complete.
  • Checked against NCSES. Each build compares 195 national totals with the tables NCSES publishes (NSF 26-304, Tables 2 and 12). All match exactly; the build stops if any do not.
  • Two survey forms. Institutions with $1 million or more in R&D answer the standard form; smaller ones answer a short form with fewer questions. Measures available only on the standard form are labeled that way below, and their national totals leave out short-form institutions (about 0.1 percent of R&D).
  • Not released by institution. NCSES does not publish, for individual institutions, how institution funds split into institutionally financed research, cost sharing and unrecovered indirect costs, or how much indirect cost was recovered. Those appear on this site only as national totals from the published tables.
  • Personnel counts are released from FY 2022 onward.
  • Institution type comes from the 2021 Carnegie Classification, held fixed for every year so a group means the same institutions throughout. HERD reporting units without a usable IPEDS ID (combined campuses, system offices, research institutes) are matched by a hand-checked crosswalk. Seven independent research institutes and three system offices, which Carnegie does not classify, are labeled by HERD Explorer.
  • Imputed values. NCSES imputes some values for nonresponse; the survey-values download keeps NCSES's status code (i = imputed, n = not available) for every value.
  • Dollars are nominal (not adjusted for inflation).

How projections are made

A projection is a best guess from past trends, not a forecast. It may not reflect what actually happens. It cannot see policy changes, agency budgets, survey revisions or an institution's own plans.

  • What is modeled. Year-to-year growth in each institution's spending (on the log scale), for nine measures: total R&D, each funding source, and federal R&D from HHS (including NIH), NSF and DOD.
  • Pooling across institutions. Each institution's expected growth is pulled toward the growth of its institution type (2021 Carnegie) and its state, a hierarchical model with random effects fit with the R package mgcv. Institutions with short or noisy histories lean most on their group.
  • Shared shocks. Good and bad years hit many institutions at once. The model estimates how large those shared year shocks are, nationally and within each institution type, and simulates new ones for future years. That is why a group's total has a realistic range rather than an overly narrow one.
  • Size matters. Small institutions' spending swings far more from year to year than large ones', so the year-to-year noise is estimated as a function of institution size.
  • Simulation. Each year the model is refit and 200 possible futures are simulated for every institution, starting from its last reported value. The site adds those futures within whatever group you pick and shows the middle 80% and 90% of the results as shaded bands. Institutions too new or too sparse to model are held at their last reported value.
  • Checked against the past. The model was fit on data through 2018, 2019, 2020 and 2021 in turn and asked to predict the next three years, which had already happened. It was scored with the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS, lower is better), which rewards projections that are both accurate and honest about their uncertainty, and compared with simpler approaches. Its ranges were widened until about 80% and 90% of the real outcomes fell inside the 80% and 90% bands.
